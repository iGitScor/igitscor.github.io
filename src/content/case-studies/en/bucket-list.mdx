## Why I built it

I built Bucket List to keep track of two things: what I have achieved, and what I still dream of doing.

I also wanted to improve my skills in machine learning, on a small scope. A list is a good playground for that: sorting items by theme is a real machine-learning task, but small enough to run entirely in the browser. So I set myself one rule: the list never leaves the browser.

<Callout title="What is a classifier?">

A program that puts things into boxes. Here, it reads each item of your list, such as “see the northern lights”, and decides which theme it belongs to, such as travel or nature.

</Callout>

## What I built

- **The list itself**, in French, to filter, explore and track. You can import almost any list: a spreadsheet export, a Markdown checklist, or one item per line. The app works out which column holds what.
- **A hidden story view**: a 2D notebook where the list becomes themed islands, and the sky follows the hour as you scroll through the day.
- **A classifier in three steps**, from the simplest to the most accurate, all running in the browser.

## How it works

**Three steps, cheapest first.** A list of keywords answers instantly. Then a light model, shipped with the site, answers in milliseconds. The full model is much bigger: it takes seconds to minutes, so it only comes later.

**A small model, trained from a big one.** The light model learned from the full one (multilingual-e5-small) by copying its answers on a sample of French and English Wikipedia. The training runs in about 15 minutes on a laptop.

<Callout title="Embeddings and distillation, in two sentences each">

An embedding turns a sentence into a list of numbers, so that sentences with close meanings get close numbers. That is how the app can tell that “climb Kilimanjaro” and “hike in the Alps” share a theme.

Distillation is a big model teaching a small one. The small model learns to give the same answers on many examples, and keeps most of the skill at a fraction of the size.

</Callout>

**Measured, not guessed.** I tested the three steps on 757 items the light model had never seen:

| Step | Agrees with the full model | Matches the list sorted by hand |
|---|---|---|
| Keywords | 72.0% | 78.2% |
| Light model | 77.5% | 79.5% |
| Full model | — | 86.0% |

<Callout title="How to read this table">

The first column compares each step with the full model. The second, which matters more, compares it with the way a person sorted the same items by hand.

</Callout>

**The full model only comes when the device can afford it:** when the browser is idle, online, on a fast connection, and has enough free space. Once it is ready, the lists are sorted again, and your own corrections are kept.

**The same result on every device.** The same list gives the same islands on my phone and on my laptop, and tests check it.

<Callout title="Why does the same result on every device matter?">

Some steps involve chance, such as how the islands are laid out. If each browser rolled its own dice, the same list could look different on your phone and on your laptop. Using the list’s own fingerprint as the dice makes the result repeatable.

</Callout>

**Stored nowhere else.** Lists, corrections and scores stay in the browser.

## What was hard

- Choosing what to measure. Agreeing with the big model looks good, but what counts is matching how a person sorts. On that, the light model is only a little better than keywords.
- Getting the same result everywhere: I had to pin every version, of the model, its weights and the engine.
- The host refuses files over 25 MiB, and the engine that runs the full model is bigger, so it loads from a CDN instead.
- Some rules should not depend on a model: personal items always go to a locked island, whatever the classifier says.
- Cutting scope. My first version was a 3D world. I dropped it for the list and a simple 2D story view, and the app is better for it.
