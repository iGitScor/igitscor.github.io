## Why I built it

I'm a Flash Invaders player. The game is about photographing the mosaics that the street artist Invader has placed in cities around the world.

The raw map of every invader exists already, but that is not what I wanted. I wanted two things: a route to collect the ones I'm missing, and the number of invaders I walked right past during a walk without flashing them.

## What I built

- **My gallery on the map**: my flashes, shown over a map of 4,415 invaders in 89 cities.
- **Missed invaders**: import a walk recorded on Strava or Apple Health, and see every invader you passed within 50 metres without flashing it.
- **A route planner**: give it a distance, and it picks the invaders worth a detour, puts them in a good order, and exports the walk as a GPX file or a Google Maps link.
- **A hunt generator** that turns a few invaders into a treasure hunt, with riddles about the neighbourhood.

There is no server behind it: the app is a static site, and your walks and settings stay in your browser.

## How it works

**Finding the invaders you walked past.** The map is cut into squares of about 111 metres, and each point of your walk only looks at the squares around it.

<Callout title="Why a grid?">

A long walk has thousands of GPS points. Checking each one against 4,415 invaders means millions of comparisons. Instead, the map is cut into squares, and each invader is filed under its square. For each point of the walk, the app only looks in the squares around it: a handful of invaders instead of thousands.

</Callout>

**Choose first, then order.** Google Maps accepts at most 8 stops, so the planner first chooses which invaders to visit, starting with the nearest ones, then adds any that cost less than 150 metres of detour. Then it puts them in order and improves that order until the walk stops crossing itself. Walking directions come from OSRM.

<Callout title="What are cheapest insertion and 2-opt?">

Cheapest insertion builds the walk one stop at a time, slipping each new stop in wherever it adds the least distance. 2-opt then tidies up: wherever the walk crosses itself, it reverses the part in between, which always makes it shorter. Or-opt does the same job by moving short runs of stops elsewhere in the walk.

</Callout>

**Riddles from open data.** The hunt generator describes each place using OpenStreetMap, Wikidata and Wikipedia, and picks from more than 40 kinds of riddles. A hunt of five invaders takes 30 to 50 seconds the first time, and under 2 seconds once the places are cached.

<Callout title="What is a cache?">

A copy of an answer kept for later. The first time the app asks the open-data services about a place, it waits for them. Then it keeps the answer for a week, so the next hunt in the same area is almost instant.

</Callout>

**Data from the community.** The list of invaders comes from a dataset maintained by other players, and the metro stations from OpenStreetMap. A script refreshes each of them.

## What was hard

- The 8-stop limit of Google Maps decided the whole design: I couldn't just “solve the route”, I had to choose the stops first, then order them.
- I expected to need a clever algorithm. A simple one, then tidying up the crossings, turned out to be enough for walks of this size.
- The free services I rely on limit how often you can call them. Caching the answers mattered more than the algorithm.
- Relying on community data means keeping it in sync myself: the update scripts are part of the product.
